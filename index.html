Emergency: the first minute

What to actually do — not what feels dramatic — for the situations classmates run into most.

Fainting (someone loses consciousness briefly)

  1. Lay them flat and raise their legs slightly if you can — don't prop them upright.
  2. Check they're breathing normally and loosen anything tight around the neck.
  3. If they don't wake within a minute, or fall and hit their head, call 119.
  4. Once awake, let them rest lying down for several minutes before sitting up.
Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0 FR (shown lying down; for fainting keep legs raised, not on the side)

Seizures

  1. Clear the area of anything they could hit. Do not hold them down or restrain their movements.
  2. Never put anything in their mouth.
  3. Time the seizure. Once it stops, roll them onto their side (recovery position).
  4. Call 119 if it lasts more than 5 minutes, repeats, or they don't regain awareness.
Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0 FR — recovery position: on their side, top leg bent for support

Nosebleeds

  1. Sit forward, not back — leaning back sends blood down the throat.
  2. Pinch the soft part of the nose firmly for a full 10 minutes without checking early.
  3. Seek care if it hasn't stopped after 20 minutes, or followed a hard head injury.

CPR & AED

Based on AHA hands-only CPR guidance for bystanders without medical training.

What each one actually is

CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation) keeps blood moving to the brain when someone's heart has stopped. An AED (automated external defibrillator) is a device that can shock the heart back into a normal rhythm — it talks you through every step and won't shock someone who doesn't need it.

If someone collapses and isn't breathing normally

  1. Call or shout for someone to call 119 immediately. Send someone to find an AED.
  2. Push hard and fast on the center of the chest — about 5–6 cm deep, 100–120 pushes per minute (the tempo of "Stayin' Alive").
  3. Let the chest rise fully between pushes. Don't stop until 119 arrives or an AED is ready.
  4. If an AED arrives, turn it on and follow its voice instructions exactly — it guides every step.
Photo: U.S. Air Force, public domain

Choking (Heimlich maneuver)

Ask "Are you choking?" first — if they can still cough, talk, or breathe, let them keep coughing. Only act if they can't.

  1. Have someone call 119.
  2. Stand slightly behind and to the side. Give 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
  3. If that doesn't work, give 5 abdominal thrusts: fist just above the navel, grab it with your other hand, and pull sharply inward and upward.
  4. Keep alternating 5 back blows / 5 abdominal thrusts until the object comes out or they lose consciousness — then start CPR.
Photo: Wikimedia Commons, public domain — fist just above the navel, thrust inward and upward

Where AEDs usually are in Korea

Subway stations, large office buildings, schools, and gyms are required to have one, usually marked with a green heart-and-lightning-bolt sign near the entrance or by elevators. Knowing your school's AED location before an emergency is part of this project's workshop.

When to seek help

Matching the situation to the right level of care.

Call 119 (ambulance/fire) if:

Someone is unconscious, not breathing normally, having chest pain, having a severe allergic reaction, bleeding heavily, or showing sudden stroke signs (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech — act on any one of these immediately).

Go to an ER (응급실) if:

Symptoms are severe or getting worse fast, but the person can still travel safely — a bad allergic reaction that's stabilizing, a deep cut that needs stitches, a suspected fracture, high fever with confusion.

An urgent-care clinic or your regular doctor is enough for:

Ongoing but non-severe symptoms — a cold that's lasted a week, a minor sprain, a rash, a prescription refill.

Healthcare basics in Korea

The system, in plain language, for someone using it for the first time.

119

Korea's free emergency dispatch number for ambulance and fire. Operators can connect you to English interpretation — say "English, please" as soon as they answer.

Triage

ERs treat by severity, not arrival order. A patient who arrives after you but is more critical will be seen first — this is normal and not a mistake.

응급실 vs. 병원 vs. 의원

응급실 (ER) is for emergencies only. 병원 (hospital) usually means a larger facility with multiple departments. 의원 (clinic) is a smaller, single-doctor practice for everyday, non-urgent care — this is where most check-ups and prescriptions happen.

How booking usually works

  1. Call the hospital's international center directly, or use its English website/contact form — most don't take booking through general search or maps apps.
  2. Have ready: your name, date of birth, nationality, phone number, and (if you have one) your Alien Registration Number.
  3. If you have Korean National Health Insurance, ask to be booked with Family Medicine first — it's usually the required first stop for insurance coverage.
  4. If you don't have insurance, ask about the self-pay rate for foreign patients before your visit.

What this page is — and isn't

This is a directory and a how-to, not a booking system. We don't take reservations or connect to any hospital's system — each hospital has its own process, and actually booking on their behalf would need a direct partnership with each one. Always confirm details on the hospital's own site before you go.

Health misinformation

Common things students believe that don't match CDC / AHA / WHO guidance.

MythTilt your head back for a nosebleed.
FactLean forward. Tilting back lets blood run down the throat.
MythHold a seizing person down so they "stop shaking."
FactNever restrain them — clear the space around them instead.
MythYou need mouth-to-mouth to do CPR.
FactFor an untrained bystander, hands-only chest compressions are the AHA-recommended default.
MythA fever needs to be brought down as fast as possible.
FactFever is the body's response fighting infection; treat the discomfort, and seek care for very high or prolonged fever, not the number itself.
